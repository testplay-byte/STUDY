## 6.5 Homogeneous Equations in two Variables

Let $f(x, y) = 0$ be any equation in two variables $x$ and $y$. The equation $f(x, y) = 0$ is called a homogeneous equation of degree $n$ if

$$f(kx, ky) = k^n f(x, y) \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (1)$$

where $k$ is any real number and $n$ is a positive integer.

### 6.5.1 Homogeneous Linear Equations in two Variables

An equation of the form $ax + by = 0$ is called a homogeneous linear equation in two variables and always passes through origin.

If we take $n = 1$ in equation (1), then we get:

$$f(kx, ky) = k f(x, y) \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (2)$$

Equation (2) is called homogeneous linear equation in two variables. Consider:

$$f(x, y) = ax + by = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (3)$$

Replacing $x$ by $kx$ and $y$ by $ky$, we have:

$$f(kx, ky) = akx + bky = k(ax + by) = k f(x, y) \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (4)$$

From (4), it is clear that equation (3) is homogeneous linear equation in two variables.

For example, $x - 4y = 0$ is a homogeneous linear equation in two variables $x$ and $y$.

> **Key Facts**
>
> In the homogeneous system of equations, no equation has a constant term. A homogeneous linear system may have one or infinitely many solutions. But it has at least one trivial solution always.

### 6.5.2 Non-Homogeneous Linear Equation

An equation of the form $ax + by + c = 0$ is called a non-homogeneous linear equation in two variables.

For example, $2x - 5y + 7 = 0$ is a non-homogeneous linear equation in two variables $x$ and $y$.

### 6.5.3 Joint Equation

Consider two straight lines represented by:

$$a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (5)$$
$$a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (6)$$

Multiplying equations (5) and (6), we have:

$$(a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1)(a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2) = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (7)$$

Equation (7) is called joint equation and can be re-written as:

$$ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (8)$$

Equation (8) represents a pair of lines and can be resolved back into two linear equations.

If we put $c_1 = c_2 = 0$ in equations (5) and (6), then:

$$a_1 x + b_1 y = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (9)$$
$$a_2 x + b_2 y = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (10)$$

Equations (9) and (10) represent a system of homogeneous linear equations in two variables.

Multiplying (9) and (10), we get:

$$(a_1 x + b_1 y)(a_2 x + b_2 y) = 0 \quad \ldots\ldots\ldots (11)$$
